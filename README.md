# Who Is the Author?
In one of my previous AI film projects, *[Antipode](https://youtu.be/cndMIjVstkw)* (Zhang, 2026), I tried to make the form of the work become part of the question it actually asks. The film was generated entirely with AI, yet it explores AI ethics, creation, and authorship, forming a loop in which AI examines itself. It unfolds through two narratives. In the light, there is a director stands on a stage and calmly condemns AI for contaminating and desecrating art, starting such a kind of digital witch hunt. In the dark, mirrors, cracks, and whispers intertwine while images collapse and reform within themselves. As the two narratives move closer together, the audience discovers that the director who began the judgement is also AI—or perhaps a performance generated by AI from human language, psychology, and fear.

This project made me ask seriously that if I direct a system that produces the work, whose work is it? My answer is neither that AI is “only a tool” nor that AI is the sole author. I see the film as a collaboration between AI and myself. However, this collaboration is not equal, and it does not mean that I can hand over all responsibility to AI.

The framework of the film was not a finished script that I first wrote independently and then asked AI to execute. Instead, everything from the initial concept to the way the narrative unfolds, for instance, the characters’ language, and the atmosphere of the images gradually took shape through multiple rounds of conversation, generation, selection, and rejection between AI and me. AI suggested visual associations and narrative directions that I had not previously imagined, and some unexpected outputs also forced me to reconsider my own ideas. In this sense, AI did more than replace the technical work that might otherwise involve 3D software, live-action filming, or post-production tools. It directly responded to my inputs, but what it could and could not generate also shaped my sense of the forms the work might take.

However, AI cannot decide the work’s ultimate meaning for me. For example, it can generate an image that looks powerful, but it cannot judge whether that image actually serves the theme itself. It can imitate a tone critical of AI, but it cannot judge whether that criticism is simply another beautiful yet empty gesture. What the film ultimately criticises, which outputs I keep, and where I want the audience to feel uncomfortable still depend on choices I make based on my experience and accumulated knowledge.which is more important is that I must take responsibility for those choices as the creator. AI has no personal experience, and it does not bear the consequences if a work is misunderstood, harms someone, or repeats an existing bias.

However, this responsibility does not mean that I have complete control over all the direction of the work. In the film, the director who judges AI eventually becomes the object of judgement. During the creative process, I realised that I was also inside a similar loop. I thought I was using AI, but its language patterns, visual habits, and cultural biases from its training data were also shaping my judgement. It might present a particular visual style as a “natural” choice or a particular emotion as “cinematic”. I must learn to recognise these tendencies rather than accept them wholesale without thinking.

At present, I only partly understand how AI systems work. I know how to use prompts, reference images, repeated generation, and selection to bring the final generated results closer to my intention. But I do not fully understand the underlying code, training data, or computational structure of the model. This partial understanding allows me to create, but it also means that I often have to infer the process from the output rather than predict exactly how the system will respond.

If I understood AI more deeply, I do not think the answer about authorship would completely change. AI would still be an important collaborator because its surprises and limitations genuinely shape the work. But my contribution would become more active and precise. I would be better able to anticipate the system’s tendencies, design the conditions of generation, and distinguish between results that came from my decisions and results that merely came from the model’s habits. Understanding the technology would not give me absolute control again. Instead, it would help me see more clearly where my control ends and where the system’s influence begins.
