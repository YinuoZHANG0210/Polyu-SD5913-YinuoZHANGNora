# Who Is the Author?

## Antipode: AI Examining Itself

In one of my previous AI film projects, *[Antipode](https://youtu.be/cndMIjVstkw)* (Zhang, 2026), I tried to make the form of the work become part of the question it asks. The film was generated entirely with AI, yet it explores AI ethics, creation, and authorship, forming a loop in which AI examines itself. It unfolds through two narratives. In the light, a director stands on a stage and calmly condemns AI for contaminating and desecrating art, sparking a digital witch hunt. In the dark, mirrors, cracks, and whispers intertwine while images collapse and reform within themselves. As the two narratives move closer together, the audience discovers that the director leading the condemnation is also AI—or perhaps a performance generated by AI from human language, psychology, and fear.

In the online description of *The Art of Code*, [Beattie (n.d.)](https://dylanbeattie.net/talks/the-art-of-code.html) describes code written simply to explore whether something is possible. I see *Antipode* in a similar spirit: it explores the artistic possibilities of creating with AI. This gives me a reason to learn programming: I want to design new rules for generation, with those rules becoming part of the work itself.

## Shared Authorship

This project made me seriously consider the question: if I direct a system that produces the work, whose work is it? My answer is neither that AI is “only a tool” nor that AI is the sole author. **I see the film as a collaboration between AI and myself.** However, this collaboration is not equal, and it does not mean that I can hand over all responsibility to AI.

The framework of the film was not a finished script that I first wrote independently and then asked AI to execute. Instead, the concept, narrative, characters’ language, and visual atmosphere gradually took shape through multiple rounds of conversation, generation, selection, and rejection between AI and me. AI suggested visual associations and narrative directions that I had not previously imagined, and some unexpected outputs also forced me to reconsider my own ideas. In this sense, AI did more than replace the technical work that might otherwise involve 3D software, live-action filming, or post-production tools. It directly responded to my inputs, but what it could and could not generate also shaped my sense of the forms the work might take.

In Note G, [Lovelace (1843, p. 722)](https://www.hist-math.fr/textes/Lovelace1843_SketchAnalytical.pdf#page=57) suggests that reorganising mathematical knowledge for machine execution can reveal relationships in a new light and influence scientific thinking. She also denies that the Analytical Engine originates anything independently. Her discussion concerns the Analytical Engine, not present-day generative AI. I connect her account of this reciprocal influence to my own process: working with AI helped me reformulate my ideas and encounter directions I had not anticipated. This supports my understanding of collaboration, although my decision to call AI a co-creator goes beyond her argument.

## Responsibility and the Limits of Control

However, I do not treat AI as the final judge of the work’s meaning. For example, it can generate an image that looks powerful, but I still need to decide whether that image serves my theme. It can imitate a tone critical of AI, but I must consider whether that criticism expresses my position or is simply a beautiful yet empty gesture. What the film ultimately criticises, which outputs I keep, and where I want the audience to feel uncomfortable still depend on choices I make based on my experience and accumulated knowledge. **I must take responsibility for those choices as the creator**, including how I respond if the work is misunderstood, harms someone, or repeats an existing bias.

Yet this responsibility does not mean that I have complete control over the direction of the work. In the film, the director who judges AI eventually becomes the object of judgement. During the creative process, I realised that I was also inside a similar loop. Ihde’s account of technological mediation, as explained by [Verbeek (2001, p. 128)](https://www.utwente.nl/en/psts/documents/ihde.pdf#page=6), describes how technologies transform perception by amplifying some aspects of experience and reducing others. Applying this idea to my film, I recognise that AI may make certain lighting effects or emotions easier to generate, encouraging me to see them as a “natural” or “cinematic” direction. My judgement remains active, but the medium helps shape the conditions in which I judge. I must learn to recognise these tendencies rather than accept them wholesale without thinking.

## What Understanding the System Changes

At present, I only partly understand how AI systems work. I know how to use prompts, reference images, repeated generation, and selection to bring the results closer to my intention. But I do not fully understand the underlying code, training data, or computational structure of the model. This partial understanding allows me to create, but it also means that I often have to infer the process from the output rather than predict exactly how the system will respond.

If I understood AI more deeply, I do not think the answer about authorship would completely change. AI would still be an important collaborator because its surprises and limitations genuinely shape the work. But **my contribution could become more active and precise.** I would hope to become better able to anticipate the system’s tendencies, design the conditions of generation, and distinguish between results that came from my decisions and results that merely came from the model’s habits. Understanding the technology would not give me absolute control. Instead, I hope it would help me see more clearly where my control ends and where the system’s influence begins. For me, learning programming is a way to question and revise the rules of generation, even when AI helps write the code.
